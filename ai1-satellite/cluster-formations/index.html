How should AI compute satellites fly together?

Six ways to arrange a cluster of ~70 m AI compute satellites riding the day–night line. Each satellite moves on its own orbit, so every shape breathes, drifts or collapses over the 96-minute lap. The view and the scores below come from the Clohessy–Wiltshire equations of relative motion, with the Sun placed where it really sits for a dawn-dusk orbit.

What are we optimizing?

The real target is useful compute per kilogram launched, over the satellite's whole life. Every layout carries the same chips, so the compute each chip gets from a watt is fixed. The formation decides how much sunlight is collected, how well the satellites can share work as one computer, and how much fuel and risk that costs. Pick a goal and the table re-ranks.

Safety is never traded. Under every goal, a layout is ruled out if two satellites come within one span of each other, or if its laser network breaks apart.

Is the flat disc the best disc?

Inside the flat disc, satellites do move relative to each other. The outline stays fixed: an oval twice as long along-track as it is tall. Inside it, every satellite circles the centre once per orbit, all in the same plane. Because those circles are stretched 2:1, neighbours slide from apart to and back twice per orbit. The outer satellites move fastest, up to about relative to the centre. Every laser has to keep re-pointing, but no two paths ever cross.

This is the same motion as Google's Suncatcher cluster. Satellites there don't swap places either: each keeps the same neighbours, and the shape breathes twice per orbit, so a different satellite sits at the tips of the oval each time. Google draws it in a frame fixed to the stars, where the whole oval also turns once per orbit. Switch the close-up to Fixed to the stars and follow S1 to see it their way.

It is not the most compact disc. Tilt the disc toward the Sun and the stretch shrinks. At 60° every satellite flies a true circle, the gaps never change, and the cluster is half the size. The catch is the seasons. At one solstice the Sun sits only 59° above the orbit's plane, and then it shines along a steeply tilted disc and satellites shade each other.

What the physics says

The orbit faces the Sun. In a dawn-dusk orbit the orbital plane follows the day–night line, so sunlight strikes that plane nearly square on. A neighbour beside you in the plane leaves your light alone. A neighbour off the plane, toward the Sun, sits in your sunlight.

Altitude differences drift. A satellite only 100 m higher circles more slowly and falls behind by about 940 m every orbit. Holding a fixed radial stack would take continuous thrust.

The free shapes are ellipses. A satellite that starts slightly off the reference orbit traces a 2:1 ellipse around it, twice as long along-track as it is tall. Satellites placed on nested ellipses with the same phase rotate together like a wheel and never cross.

Cross-track offsets cross twice per orbit. Offsets made by tilting the orbit all pass through zero at the same moment, unless they are paired with an along-track or radial offset.

How each score is worked out

  • Closest approach is the minimum centre-to-centre distance of any pair over one orbit. Red below one satellite span, amber below two.
  • Panel shading treats each array as a sun-facing disc the size of the satellite and measures how much of it a sunward neighbour covers. Penumbra and Earth albedo are ignored.
  • Laser network fixes one link plan for the whole orbit, picking the pairs whose worst-case distance is shortest, up to the terminal limit. Hops is the longest shortest path across the cluster.
  • Δv to hold shape is a rough yearly budget. In-plane ellipses use the ~3 m/s per year per km figure from the Suncatcher study. Cross-track motion made by inclination offsets is charged for J2 differential precession. A radial stack is charged for the thrust that stops it drifting.

What this leaves out

  • Orbits are circular and two-body, with J2 only in the Δv estimate. Drag is ignored because identical satellites feel it equally.
  • AI1 dimensions are public estimates. The satellite count, terminal count and link range are assumptions to explore, not SpaceX figures.
  • Radiator view, line-of-sight blocking by a third satellite, and plume impingement are not modelled.
